# Johnny Meier Lineage, Round 3: Confirmations and Origins (October 2026)

This round had three goals:

1. Turn "probable" links into "confirmed" ones.
2. Trace the Meier surname line as far back as the records go.
3. Pin every family line to a specific town and specific people in Germany, Italy, England, France and Ireland.

It builds on the Round 1 and Round 2 reports and does not repeat them.

**How confident each finding is:**

- **Confirmed** means I read the original record image, or two independent records agree.
- **Probable** means strong evidence that still lacks one deciding document.
- **Speculative** means a lead only.

**Where the details are:** full sources and record links are in `research/notes/round3/`. There is one file each for the Meier line, the other German lines, the Italian lines, the English and French lines, and the Irish and Pennsylvania lines. Living people were not researched.

## 1. The short version

- **The Meier line is now proven, generation by generation, from your grandfather back to a carpenter born in the 1700s.**
  - It runs James C. → William F. → Henry J. → Friedrich → Heinrich → Michael Mayer.
  - The family came from **Munzingen**, a wine village just west of Freiburg im Breisgau in Baden. It was not Kirchzarten.
  - Every US link comes from census pages and marriage applications. Every German link comes from the original Munzingen civil registers.
- **The Birkenmayers were from Munzingen too.** Maria Anna Birkenmayer, Friedrich's wife, was born there in 1837. Her parents and grandparents are confirmed there as well, back to a Johann Ev. Birkenmayer born in 1771.
- **Your Italian lines now reach the early 1800s in four towns.** The Guardia Lombardi line reaches a couple married before about 1796, Pietro di Marino and Felicita Magnotta. The other three towns are Bisaccia, Dasà and San Mango d'Aquino.
- **Your Italian great-great-grandmother was a foundling.** Angelica Ferrantino, Mollie's mother, was left as a baby at **Sant'Angelo dei Lombardi** around 1873. Her parents are unknown.
- **Your English roots are in specific parishes.**
  - Tuthill: Tharston and Saxlingham, Norfolk.
  - Hammond and Paine: Lavenham and Long Melford, Suffolk.
  - Topping: Totternhoe, Bedfordshire.
  - Horton: Mowsley, Leicestershire.
- **Your French roots are Huguenot, from Saintonge on the Atlantic coast.**
  - The Lamoureux were from Meschers-sur-Gironde.
  - The Mersereau, Masse and Dubois families were from Moëze, near Rochefort.
- **Your Irish roots are in Co. Tipperary.** The Heffernan and Boney families lived at Lickfinn and Mardyke in the Slieveardagh coalfield. This is now confirmed.
- **One line has to come out.** The **Brewster** ancestry, Francis and Lucy Brewster of New Haven, is probably not yours. The cousins found "via Brewster" in Round 2 go with it. One journal article still needs to be read directly before this is final.

## 5. Stories from this round

- **A pilot of the Gironde.** André Lamoureux guided ships through the dangerous mouth of the Gironde. He fled France with his family and lost a son at Bristol in 1690. He was naturalized by Act of Parliament in 1694. In 1706 the governor of New York ordered him released after he had been forced into the Royal Navy.
- **A Huguenot wedding season.** In the summer of 1693, New York's French Church married three Mersereau siblings in three weeks. Your ancestor Pierre Masse signed as witness every time, and the widowed mother Elisabet Dubois made her mark.
- **The three friars (family legend).** Jean Mersereau supposedly greeted three disguised friars with "Good evening, gentlemen", saying he knew "but one Father, who is in Heaven". When they drew hidden swords, he fought them off. This is a genuine family tradition but not proven history.
- **The water bailiff who helped found Connecticut.** In 1636 Thomas Topping reported to the Wraysbury manor court that "all was well with the water courses". Wraysbury parish contains Magna Carta Island. In 1662 he was named in King Charles II's charter for Connecticut.
- **Lavenham clothiers.** William Hammond was born in one of England's richest wool towns, and his great-grandfather was a fuller. William's wife sailed to New England in 1634 with three children, including your ancestor Sarah.
- **The reluctant bridegroom.** In 1640 Bull Smith petitioned to be released from his promise to marry Sarah Hammond. He lost, and their marriage founded the Smithtown line. In 1656 he may have sailed back from England on the same ship as the first Quaker missionaries to New England.
- **Stonemasons from a wine village.** Munzingen sits on the Tuniberg vineyards under the Counts of Kageneck, whose castle (Schloss) dates from 1672. Friedrich Mayer's next-door neighbour in Hazleton in 1870 was another Baden stone mason, a sign of a small Baden building-trades network.
- **A brother-in-law with "fever & ague".** Gabriel Birkenmayer was sick with a malaria-type fever on census day in 1880, while living with his sister's family. He and his brother Joseph later went to Sandusky, Ohio.
- **From slate picker to clerk.** In 1880 Addison Pringle's 13-year-old brother Orange picked slate in a coal breaker. Two generations later, both of your paternal grandparents' parents were clerks and bookkeepers. In 1910 the Pringle and McGuire families lived in the same small town, Larksville, 18 years before their children married.
- **A foundling's name.** "Ferrantino" was probably assigned to Angelica at the foundling wheel of Sant'Angelo dei Lombardi.
- **Shepherds and candle-makers.** The Giannettas and Fedes of Bisaccia were shepherds. In 1855 Nicola Cognetta of Dasà was styled "Mastro", a master craftsman: he was a candle-maker, making votive and church candles.
- **A Tipperary miner's son.** The Heffernans dug coal in the Slieveardagh hills of Tipperary before they dug anthracite in Luzerne County.
